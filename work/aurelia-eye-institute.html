Aurelia Eye Institute.
A hospital profile that claims nothing it cannot prove.

A 16-page institutional profile for a specialist eye hospital. Healthcare is where marketing does the most damage when it overstates, so the design brief was restraint: explain each service in the patient's words, source every medical sentence, and leave a clear space for facts only the hospital can supply.

This is a design concept, not client work. Aurelia is a fictional name; the photographs are licensed stock of people who are not clinicians or patients; services and roles are sample content. The profile says so on its cover and its credits page, and so do we.

The design

Seven decisions, most of them about restraint.

The visual language — calm, serif headlines, one warm gold accent — does its job quietly. The decisions that matter in a clinical document are about what it will and will not say.

Claim nothing unverified
No accreditations, awards, outcome figures, patient volumes, testimonials or clinician credentials — because none had been verified. A hospital profile is read by patients deciding whom to trust with their sight, and by regulators. An invented number is a liability in both directions.
Source every medical sentence
Each service overview names its general reference — the US National Eye Institute, the American Academy of Ophthalmology, the FDA — and the credits page lists the full addresses. The explanations are general, marked as not medical advice, and flagged for review by the hospital's own clinicians before publication.
Written for the patient
"A cataract is a cloudy area in the eye's natural lens." The profile explains conditions in the words a patient uses, then says what an assessment may involve. Clinical vocabulary appears where a patient will hear it in consultation, and is defined when it does.
Say what cannot be promised
"Suitability depends on individual assessment. Outcomes vary and cannot be guaranteed." Laser correction and surgery are described with their limits, because the fastest way to lose a patient's trust is to oversell the procedure they came to ask about.
Urgent symptoms, plainly
Sudden flashes, a shower of floaters, a shadow across vision, a painful red eye: the profile says these need prompt medical attention, twice, where a worried reader will find them. A brochure that makes someone wait is a design failure.
Label every image
"Illustrative photography. People shown are not staff or patients of this institution." Stock imagery is fine; stock imagery passed off as your ward and your surgeons is not. Every photograph is credited to its photographer on the last page.
Leave room for the real facts
Address, telephone, opening hours, consultant names and registrations are explicit placeholders — "to be supplied by the hospital" — rather than plausible inventions. The structure is finished; the facts are the client's to supply and verify.
What it means for you

The structure is finished. The facts are yours.

A clinic, hospital or practice brings its own name, people, photographs and verified credentials; the architecture, the tone and the discipline about claims carry over. The same approach applies to the website — see what patients check before they get in touch in our clinic website guide.

Carries over

Page architecture, service-overview format, the visit pathway, the urgent-symptoms treatment and the credits discipline.

Gets replaced

Name, identity, photography of your real premises and team, and every clinical fact — verified by your clinicians.

Also works for

Dental and aesthetic clinics, physiotherapy, diagnostics, veterinary practices — any service where trust is the product.

Print and web

The profile's structure maps directly onto a website: one page per service, a visit guide, and a consultant directory.
