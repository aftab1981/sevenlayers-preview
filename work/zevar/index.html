Case study · Jewellery ecommerce design

ZEVAR.
Jewellery, sold the way jewellery is bought.

Most jewellery goes online through a template that treats a hand-set kundan piece like a phone case. We designed the opposite: a storefront where the photograph is the interface, browsing works by occasion as well as by piece, bridal sells as a matched set, and the checkout is built around how this market actually pays. It is live, and every screen below is the shipped store.

The problem

Jewellery breaks stock ecommerce templates.

Four things about this category fight a generic store, and all four had to be designed around rather than worked around.

Photography is the whole sale

A generic grid shrinks the piece to a thumbnail beside a paragraph. Nobody buys jewellery from a paragraph.

Buyers shop by moment

The question is "what do I wear to this wedding?", never "show me your chandbali range".

Trust is the conversion problem

Imitation, plated, zircon, fine — a buyer who cannot tell which they are getting does not order.

Bridal is one purchase, not four

The biggest order in the store is a matched set, usually preceded by a conversation.

The design

Eight decisions, and the reason for each.

None of these are stylistic. Each one exists because of something specific about selling jewellery online in this market.

Occasion before category
The first thing under the fold is three occasions, not a product grid. Nobody wakes up needing a chandbali — they need something for a wedding, or for the office. Occasion and category then work as two independent filters, because a shopper preparing for a mehndi wants pieces across four categories at once and a store organised by category alone cannot show them that.
The vocabulary of the category
Jhumka, chandbali, tops and bali, mala, choker, meena, kara, kundan, polki, latkan. Not "earrings" and "necklaces". Using the words a customer says out loud is what makes a shop feel built by someone who knows the trade — and it is what they type into a search box.
A box, not a basket
The cart is "your jewellery box". Small word, real effect: it frames the purchase as something being assembled and kept rather than a transaction being totalled. Line items carry the piece's own thumbnail and stock reference, and the subtotal says outright that shipping and COD charges follow at checkout.
Bridal is a set, and a conversation
The highest-value order in the store gets its own band. Necklace, jhumka, tikka and ring arrive as one box rather than four line items, and a free WhatsApp styling call opens the order — because nobody spends a wedding budget through a checkout form alone.
Honest about what it is
Gold-tone and antique plating rather than solid gold. American-diamond zircon rather than diamond. A nickel-light base, and a care card in the box. Specificity is the trust argument in imitation jewellery: vague stores get returns, specific ones get repeat orders. The brand line — beautiful jewellery, never pretending to be gold — is the positioning, not a disclaimer.
Built for how the market pays
Rupee pricing, a free-shipping threshold and Cash on Delivery stated above everything else, because in this market those are the two facts that decide whether a stranger buys. Most templates assume a card-first checkout and bolt COD on afterwards; here it is in the design from the first sketch.
Two scripts, one wordmark
ZEVAR in Latin and زیور in Urdu, set together and balanced. The brand reads correctly to both halves of its audience without one being a translation of the other.
The phone is the design
Most of this market shops on a mid-range Android over patchy data, so the phone layout was designed first and the desktop adapted from it. The promise bar, the headline scale and the occasion chips all survive the narrow viewport intact.
What it means for you

The design is a pattern, not a one-off.

The thinking above is what took the time; the pieces, palette and photography are what make it one brand rather than another. Rebuilding it around a different jewellery business costs a fraction of designing one from nothing — and the same argument holds for any sector whose products refuse to sit in a stock template.

Carries over

Occasion-and-category filtering, the box, set merchandising, the materials pattern, the COD-first checkout.

Gets rebuilt

Wordmark, palette, typography, photography direction and the catalogue itself.

Also works for

Any catalogue where the photograph sells and stock categories fail — apparel, furniture, artisan goods.

Either platform

Shopify where you want stock and payments handled for you; custom where the merchandising fights a template.
